1 Answer[Author] made this to [purpose word] [audience] that…
2 InferI can infer that…
3 EvidenceSource B says, "…"
4 ExplainThis shows that…
5 OutcomeThe author wants [audience] to [do something].
Purpose words: convince persuade encourage warn reassure justify criticise blame
A better version of your Weighted Assessment 2 answer: Mr Khaw made this speech to convince Singaporeans that the route going around the nature reserve is not a good idea. He says the longer route adds six minutes, and anything over five minutes counts as a disruption. This shows the longer route would make commuters frustrated. He wants Singaporeans to accept the shorter route under the reserve.
Question 3 (7 marks) Comparison "How similar or different are Sources C and D?"
1 AnswerSources C and D are similar (or different) in…
2 PointBoth sources show that… (or: Source C says… but Source D says…)
3 EvidenceSource C says, "…". Source D says, "…"
4 ExplainThis shows that…
To score higher: give one similarity and one difference, then end with "Overall, they are more similar (or different) because…". Compare the message, not the type of source.
Question 4 (7 marks) Reliability "How reliable is Source E…?"
1 AnswerSource E is (not) reliable about…
2 InferSource E tells me that…
3 EvidenceSource E says, "…"
4 Cross-checkThis is supported (or contradicted) by Source __, which says "…"
5 AuthorSource E is from ___, who may want to ___.
If another source agrees with it, it is reliable
If another source disagrees with it, it is not reliable
If the author has a hidden agenda (to look good, sell something or blame someone), it is not reliable
Question 5 (10 marks) Evaluative "How far do the sources show that…?"
1 Summary sentenceSources __ and __ support that [copy the question]. However, Sources __ and __ do not.
2 One paragraph for each source (all five)Source A supports that… Source A says, "…". Therefore, this shows that…
3 ConclusionOverall, I think [your answer] because…

Section B: how to write PEEL

PPoint Answer the question in one sentence. "One way (or impact) is…"
EExplain Say how or why it happens. "This is because…"
EExample A real Singapore example from your notes. "For example,…"
LLink Tie it back to the question's words. "Therefore,… [use the question's words]"
Question 6: Opinion (7 marks)One PEEL paragraph
  • It starts with "In your opinion…" and asks for a reason, a suggestion or an impact.
  • Pick one point you know well and go deep. One strong paragraph beats two thin ones.
  • Say "I think…" in your Point.
Question 7: Explanation (8 marks)Two PEEL paragraphs
  • It names two things (for example, race and nationality). Write one PEEL paragraph for each.
  • Use a different example in each paragraph.
  • Never use the extracts as examples. Use the examples in Sections 4 to 7.

Chapter 5: what makes Singapore diverse

Race and ethnicity Religion Nationality Socio-economic status

For each one, learn the meaning, how it creates diversity and the examples. Your Chapter 5 class notes were not in the files used for these notes, so check these against them.

Race and ethnicity
Meaning: Race is about shared ancestry and looks. Ethnicity is about shared culture, such as language, food and customs.
How it creates diversity: Singapore has four main groups: Chinese, Malay, Indian and Others. Each has smaller groups too, such as Hokkien and Cantonese Chinese, or Tamil and Punjabi Indians.
Examples
Ethnic Integration Policy (EIP): the Housing and Development Board (HDB) limits how many of each race can live in a block, so neighbours come from different races.
Group Representation Constituencies (GRCs): each team must include at least one minority candidate, so minorities have a voice in Parliament.
Public holidays: Chinese New Year, Hari Raya Puasa and Deepavali are holidays for everyone.
Religion
Meaning: A set of beliefs and practices about God or gods and how to live.
How it creates diversity: Singapore has Buddhists, Christians, Muslims, Taoists and Hindus, and about 1 in 5 people have no religion. Each religion has its own festivals, food rules (such as halal or vegetarian), dress and places of worship.
Examples
Places of worship side by side: on Waterloo Street, a Buddhist temple (Kwan Im Thong Hood Cho) stands next to a Hindu temple (Sri Krishnan).
Inter-Religious Organisation (IRO): leaders of different religions work together to build understanding.
Maintenance of Religious Harmony Act: action can be taken against anyone who stirs up hatred between religions.
Nationality
Meaning: The country a person legally belongs to.
How it creates diversity: Singapore has citizens, permanent residents (PRs), and foreigners who work or study here. Some foreigners later become citizens through naturalisation (Chapter 1).
Examples
Foreign workers: build our flats, roads and Mass Rapid Transit (MRT) lines (remember the Serangoon Gardens dormitories case study in Chapter 3).
Foreign professionals: work in hospitals, banks and research labs.
Singapore Citizenship Journey: new citizens learn about Singapore's history and values before they get citizenship.
Socio-economic status (SES)
Meaning: A person's place in society based on their income, education and job.
How it creates diversity: Families live very different lives. Some live in rental flats, others in HDB flats, condominiums or landed homes, and they may have different chances in education and work.
Examples
ComCare: government help with cash, food and bills for low-income families.
Edusave and the Ministry of Education (MOE) Financial Assistance Scheme: help students from lower-income families pay for school (links to meritocracy in Chapter 3).
Curiosity Credits (2026): youths aged 13 to 17 in self-help groups get S$500 to try hobbies such as arts, sports and technology.

Chapter 6: building harmony through interactions

Mixing with different groups can lead to sharing cultures, knowledge and skills, but also to tensions. Singapore builds harmony in two ways:

Common spacesandan open mindbuildharmony
Common spaces
Meaning: A physical or social space where people interact in activities or work together on projects.
Why it helps: People build shared experiences and memories. This creates mutual understanding, a sense of community, commitment to Singapore and trust in each other.
Examples
Places: community clubs, Residents' Networks, void decks, hawker centres.
Shared experiences: school, co-curricular activities (CCAs), Secondary 1 camp, Secondary 3 Outward Bound, Racial Harmony Day, National Day.
People's Association (PA): set up in 1960 to promote racial harmony and social cohesion. It runs about 1,800 grassroots groups, over 100 community clubs and five Community Development Councils.
New in 2026: the SG Youth Plan is setting up 12 free youth "third spaces" by the end of 2026. About 3 in 5 youths surveyed said such spaces would help them meet people from different backgrounds.
Openness
Meaning: An attitude of respecting and appreciating people who are different from you.
Why it helps: Keeping an open mind to listen to and engage others builds real understanding between people of different backgrounds. This leads to a harmonious society.
Everyday examples
Trying another culture's food at a hawker centre.
Visiting a friend's Hari Raya or Deepavali open house.
Asking a classmate why they fast during Ramadan.

Chapter 6: the benefits (positive effects)

The good side:sharing culturesandsharing know-how
Benefit 1: Cultural exchange and appreciation
What it is: Different groups share their ideas, customs and traditions.
Why it is good: Singaporeans learn to appreciate other ethnic groups and nationalities. This builds meaningful relationships and shapes our local culture and art.
Examples
ASEAN Youth Fellowship: brings together young leaders from across Southeast Asia to build partnerships and make positive changes in their communities.
Dr Iskandar Jalil's pottery: combines Japanese and Islamic styles, after he studied in Japan in the 1970s.
Fusion: Ms Oniatta Effendi's batik clothing mixes Javanese patterns with Japanese designs. Chef Willin Low mixes Singapore and European food, such as laksa pesto linguine.
Benefit 2: Exchange of knowledge and skills
What it is: People from different countries, backgrounds and experiences work together.
Why it is good: This creates new ideas and innovation. Foreign scientists share their expertise with Singaporeans, which strengthens Singapore's research.
Examples
A*STAR and Cytos Biotechnology: Singapore's Agency for Science, Technology and Research (A*STAR) worked with this Swiss company on an H1N1 flu vaccine.
Thermo Fisher Scientific: a multinational company whose local and foreign scientists do research in Singapore. During COVID-19 it helped supply PCR test kits and protective equipment.
Watch out: cultural appropriation (not "appreciation") Cultural exchange can also cause problems, such as minority cultures being replaced or absorbed by the majority. Cultural appropriation means using another culture's language, food, music or dress without permission or in an insensitive way. Example: fashion brand Max Mara was accused of copying the traditional prints of the Oma people of Laos without recognising where they came from.

Chapter 6: the challenges (negative effects)

For each challenge, learn the problem and how the government responds.

Challenge 1: Stereotypes, prejudice and discrimination
ThinkStereotype An assumption about a whole group, based on general beliefs about things such as looks, gender, age or nationality.
FeelPrejudice Negative feelings towards a group because of stereotypes, shown in insensitive or hurtful words and actions.
DoDiscrimination Openly hurting or excluding a group because of prejudice.
Xenophobia is an irrational fear of foreigners. Racism is discrimination because of race.
Why it is bad: it causes hurt, anger and distrust, which can divide society and break harmony.
How the government responds
Maintenance of Religious Harmony Act: keeps religion and politics separate, guards against foreign ideas that clash with our multi-religious society, and encourages respect and trust across religions.
Maintenance of Racial Harmony Act (passed February 2025): lets the government stop people from spreading content that harms racial harmony, and gives people who commit less serious race-related offences a chance to mend ties instead of being prosecuted.
Protection from Online Falsehoods and Manipulation Act (POFMA, 2019): Singapore's anti-fake news law. It can order false posts to be corrected, which stops lies that stir up hatred between groups.
Challenge 2: Competition for resources
Why it happens: Singapore's falling birth rate and ageing population cause a manpower shortage, so more foreigners come to work here. This creates tension over jobs and infrastructure.
Jobs: the problem
High-skilled foreigners in PMET jobs (professionals, managers, executives and technicians) compete with local graduates for the same white-collar jobs.
Lower-skilled foreign workers accept lower pay, which keeps wages low for lower-income Singaporeans.
How the government responds
SkillsFuture Singapore and Workforce Singapore: give training grants and help people retrain and find new jobs.
Fair Consideration Framework: employers must consider Singaporeans fairly before hiring foreigners.
Stricter rules: tighter Employment Pass criteria, a higher minimum salary for S Passes, and a higher levy for work permits.
Infrastructure: the problem
More people put pressure on Singapore's limited resources: crowded public transport, competition for healthcare, and the need for more housing.
How the government responds
Land Transport Master Plan 2040: more train connections, priority for buses, more cycling paths and more integrated transport hubs.
Cross Island Line: a new MRT line to reduce crowding on existing lines (you studied it in Weighted Assessment 2).

Model answer: Question 6
PIn my opinion, Singaporeans can work for the good of society by volunteering to meet the needs of groups that the government may have missed.
EThe government sets up policies and national programmes, but there are still gaps in meeting the specific needs of some groups, such as the elderly. Citizens who notice these gaps can start their own efforts, using resources from the government and community groups.
ExFor example, RSVP Singapore, the Organisation of Senior Volunteers, runs programmes such as My Buddy and Cyberguide, where volunteers befriend elderly people and teach them to use technology. Individuals also help, such as the volunteer instructor in the Age Well Everyday programme who teaches the elderly to exercise and stay active.
LTherefore, I think volunteering is the best way for Singaporeans to work for the good of society, because it fills the gaps and improves the lives of those who need help.
Another way you could use: giving feedback to the government through official channels such as REACH, or taking part in a Citizens' Jury, to influence decisions (Chapter 4).
Model answer: Question 7
PCommon spaces help to build a harmonious society because they bring people of different backgrounds together.
ECommon spaces are physical or social spaces where people of different races, religions and nationalities interact in activities or work together on projects. This creates shared experiences and memories, which build mutual understanding, a sense of community and trust.
ExFor example, the People's Association runs community clubs and Residents' Networks where residents of all backgrounds join classes and festival celebrations. In school, students bond through co-curricular activities and the Secondary 3 Outward Bound course, and from 2026 the SG Youth Plan is setting up free youth spaces where young people from different backgrounds can meet.
LTherefore, common spaces help to build a harmonious society because they turn strangers into friends and create trust between different groups.
PAn attitude of openness also helps to build a harmonious society because it makes people willing to understand those who are different from them.
EOpenness means respecting and appreciating diversity. When people keep an open mind to listen to and engage others, they build real understanding and stop believing stereotypes, so there is less prejudice.
ExFor example, Singaporeans can try food from another culture at a hawker centre, visit a friend's Hari Raya or Deepavali open house, or ask a classmate why he fasts during Ramadan instead of assuming.
LTherefore, openness helps to build a harmonious society because understanding between people of different backgrounds leads to respect and harmony.
